\section*{Appendix A: details of the empirical likelihood method for small sample inference}

The empirical likelihood procedure introduced by \citet{Amorim2018} consists of two main changes as compared to the original PIM methodology. First of all, the authors showed that the second-order bias of the parameter estimates can be reduced by replacing $\bbeta$ in $\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij};\bbeta)$ with a constant value $\bbeta_c = \mb{0}$, during the iterative procedure for solving the estimating equations in (3) of the original paper). In doing so, also the computation time is decreased as there is no need for updating $V^{-1}(g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta))$ from iteration to iteration. The resulting bias-reduced estimator can be denoted by $\bbeta_{br}$. Secondly and more importantly, the authors construct the empirical likelihood function for the PIM as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{emp_lik}
    L(\bbeta) = \max_{\boldsymbol{w}}\left\{ \prod_{i=1}^{n}{w_i} : \sum_{i=1}^{n}{w_i}=1, \sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{w_i w_j \boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{W}_i,\boldsymbol{W}_j;\bbeta)=\boldsymbol{0}} \right\},
\end{equation}
in which $\boldsymbol{w}' = (w_1,\ldots,w_n)$ corresponds to the probability vector of the multinomial distribution imposed on the $n$ sample points in $\mathcal{I}_n$ and $\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{W}_i,\boldsymbol{W}_j;\bbeta) = \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij};\bbeta)\{ I_{ij} - g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta)\}$ with $\boldsymbol{W}'_i = (Y_i,\boldsymbol{X}'_i)$. Of course, the constrained maximization involved in \eqref{emp_lik} is non-linear and computationally intensive. Therefore, following \citet{Jing2009}, the estimating equations in (3) of the original paper were rewritten as a sum of asymptotically independent terms, $\hat{\boldsymbol{H}_i}(\bbeta)$, constructed as the jacknife pseudo-values, defined as  $n\boldsymbol{U}_n(\bbeta) - (n-1) \boldsymbol{U}^{(-i)}_n(\bbeta)$, with $\boldsymbol{U}^{(-i)}_n(\bbeta)$ the estimating function calculated after removal of observation $\boldsymbol{W}_i$.  The constraint in \eqref{emp_lik} can then be replaced by $\sum_{i=1}^{n}{w_i \hat{\boldsymbol{H}_i}(\bbeta) }=\boldsymbol{0}$, which is linear in the $w_i$ and hence greatly simplifies the maximization process. This gives rise to the jacknife empirical likelihood method.  A necessary condition for the maximum of the empirical likelihood or the jackknife empirical likelihood to exist is that $\mb{0}$ is an interior point of the convex hull of the $n$ points $\hat{\boldsymbol{H}_i}(\bbeta)$. Because a maximum is often difficult to find, \citet{Chen2008} proposed to add one extra pseudo-observation, say $\hat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n+1}(\bbeta)$, so that $\mb{0}$ is always included in the resulting convex hull of $n + 1$ points. This final extension leads to the adjusted jacknife empirical likelihood (AJEL) method proposed by \citet{Amorim2018}, by replacing $\bbeta$ with $\bbeta_{br}$. The authors refer to this as the alternative bias-reduced adjusted jacknife empirical likelihood (BR-AJEL) method.

As mentioned in the main text, a nice feature of the empirical likelihood method is that it delivers an empirical likelihood ratio statistic, for which Wilks’ theorem holds \cite{Zhao2015, Wang2015, Amorim2018} so that confidence intervals can be computed from quantiles of a chi-squared distribution. Hence, the method avoids the estimation of the covariance matrix of the parameter estimators, and the resulting confidence intervals are not necessarily symmetric around the parameter estimates (in contrast to Wald confidence intervals).
