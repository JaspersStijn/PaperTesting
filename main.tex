\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

Longitudinal studies that follow two or more groups over time, whether defined by treatment assignment or environmental exposure, are fundamental for understanding how interventions and exposures affect outcomes of different types. For example, \cite{He2015} investigated the long term effectiveness of public health strategies to lower blood pressure, a continuous endpoint, while the AVERT trial discussed by \cite{Bernhardt2015Lancet}, compares stroke patients using the modified Rankin Scale, which is an ordinal measure of functional recovery. Similarly, \cite{Parzen2011} describes a longitudinal study that monitors cardiac abnormalities in children born to HIV-infected women, thus giving an example of a binary endpoint. 

The resulting longitudinal data, obtained from repeatedly measuring the same individuals on multiple occasions, are a valuable source for capturing both temporal dynamics and individual variability. In the current work, a primary main objective is to estimate a group effect by comparing two groups of individuals who have received different treatment or different exposure. In addition, since interest often extends beyond estimating main effects, a secondary aim is to investigate possible interaction effects, i.e. whether the effect of a treatment differs across subgroups or changes over time.

Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) are widely used for analyzing longitudinal data, as they can account for within-subject correlation while flexibly modeling both fixed and random effects \citep{Laird1982,fitzmaurice2011}. However, these models rely on specific assumptions that are often violated in practice, especially when dealing with non-Gaussian outcomes or complex interaction structures, potentially leading to biased or misleading inferences.

As an alternative, several non-parametric methods have been developed to analyze longitudinal or factorial repeated measures data while relaxing distributional assumptions. One such approach is the nparLD framework, which uses rank-based methods to test the main and interaction effects in longitudinal designs with repeated measures or factorial structures \cite{Noguchi2012}. Although robust and assumption-light, these methods are primarily designed for hypothesis testing, rather than estimation. Therefore, they only provide limited information on the magnitude or direction of the effects. In particular, they do not easily support interpretable covariate-adjusted effect estimates similar to those obtained from regression models.

The Probabilistic Index Model (PIM) developed by \cite{ThaDen2012} and further discussed in \cite{DenTha2015} offers a semi-parametric alternative that bridges the gap between fully parametric models like LMMs and rank-based testing procedures such as nparLD. Rather than modeling the outcome itself, the PIM models the probabilistic index (PI), which is the probability that a randomly selected individual with one covariate profile has a higher outcome than another random individual with a different profile. This approach yields a robust and interpretable effect measure that is invariant under monotonic transformations of the outcome, and it allows for covariate adjustment without requiring strong distributional assumptions. In this perspective, \cite{Jaspers2024} show how PIMs can be used for generalized pairwise comparison (GPC), even when sample sizes are small. Finally, the output of PIMs includes confidence intervals and p-values, facilitating effect size interpretation and communication in applied contexts. 

Despite its advantages, the standard PIM is restricted to settings where the original outcomes are obtained from independent observations. Therefore, they cannot be directly applied to longitudinal data, where repeated measurements within individuals induce intra-subject correlation. To address this limitation, we investigate an extension of the PIM for longitudinal settings, which accommodates within-subject correlation and enables the modeling of covariate-adjusted treatment effects, time effects and p interaction effects.

\section{Methodology}
\label{methods}

In this section, we first repeat the original PIM methodology followed by its extension towards longitudinal data. 

\subsection{Original Probabilistic Index Models}

The binary indicator $A$ is used to refer to the two groups under comparison. Without loss of generality, $A=0$ indicates the control group ($C$), and $A=1$ to the treatment or experimental group ($E$). The covariates are denoted by the vector $\boldsymbol{X}$ of length $p$. The outcome variable is denoted by $\boldsymbol{Y}$, which may be a vector of $d$ multiple outcomes. The variables $A^*$, $\boldsymbol{X}^*$ and $\boldsymbol{Y}^*$ have the same joint distribution as $A$, $\boldsymbol{X}$ and $\boldsymbol{Y}$, and they are stochastically independent of the latter. The sample space of $\boldsymbol{Y}$ is denoted by $\mathcal{Y}$ and the sample observations by $A_i$, $\boldsymbol{X}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{Y}_i$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, or with lower-case notation in case of the corresponding observed values.

Ignoring the covariates, GPC effect sizes are all related to the probability that the outcome of a subject in the experimental group ($A = 1$) is favourable compared to the outcome of a subject from the control group (A = 0). For a single outcome and under the assumption that a larger value is considered to be favourable, this probability is $P(Y < Y^*|A=0,A^*=1)$. When ties are possible (e.g. with ordinal outcomes), special attention is required for the case of $Y = Y^*$. A popular adaptation is the effect size
\[
  \Theta = P(Y < Y^*|A=0,A^*=1) + 0.5 P(Y = Y^*|A=0,A^*=1) := P(Y \preceq Y^*|A=0,A^*=1),
\]
which will be referred to as the probabilistic index (PI), but it is also known as e.g. the relative effect \cite{Brunner2021b}. In the presence of covariates, it is convenient to define the conditional versions of the GPCs by expressing the probability that an outcome from the experimental arm is favourable compared to an outcome from the control group, given that their covariates coincide. This conditional PI thus becomes $P(Y \preceq Y^*|A=0,A^*=1,\mb{X}=\mb{X^*})$. 

Upon using the notation introduced above, a probabilistic index model can be written as
\begin{equation}
  g(P(Y \preccurlyeq Y^* | A,A^*,\mb{X},\mb{X^*} ))  = \beta_0 + \beta_A (A^* - A) +  \bbeta'_{X} (\mb{X^*} - \mb{X}),
\label{final_PIM}  
\end{equation}
where $g(.)$ is a link function, $\bbeta'_{X} = (\beta_1,\cdots,\beta_p)$ is a vector with $p$ parameters, and $\beta_0$ is an offset determined by the link function. For example, for logit and probit link functions the offset is zero, while it equals 0.5 for the identity link function. The treatment effect size follows from setting $A=0$ and $A^*=1$ in model (\ref{final_PIM}):
\begin{equation}
  g(P(Y \preccurlyeq Y^* | A=0, A^*=1,\mb{X},\mb{X^*} ))  = \beta_0 + \beta_A +  \bbeta'_{X} (\mb{X^*} - \mb{X}),
\label{final_PIM2}  
\end{equation}
from which $\beta_A=g(P(Y \preccurlyeq Y^* | A=0, A^*=1,\mb{X}=\mb{X^*} ))-\beta_0$. For example, with the logit link function, we find $\beta_A=\text{logit}(P(Y \preccurlyeq Y^* | A=0, A^*=1,\mb{X}=\mb{X^*} ))$. 
The $\beta_A$ parameter is thus directly related to the conditional effect size of the treatment. 

Further on, it will be convenient to use a more efficient notation with $\bbeta = (\beta_0,\beta_A,\bbeta'_{X})$, $\boldsymbol{Z}' = (A^* - A, (\mb{X^*} - \mb{X})')$ and $\eta(\boldsymbol{Z},\bbeta) = \boldsymbol{Z}'\bbeta = \beta_0 + \beta_A (A^* - A) +  \bbeta'_{X} (\mb{X^*} - \mb{X})$ as the linear predictor. \cite{ThaDen2012} proposed to estimate the parameters by solving the estimating equations
\begin{equation}
\label{est_eq}
\boldsymbol{U}_n(\bbeta) = \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{I}_n}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij};\bbeta)\{ I_{ij} - g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta) \} } = \boldsymbol{0},    
\end{equation}
which result from rewriting the PIM expressed in \eqref{final_PIM} in terms of so-called pseudo-observations $I_{ij} = I(Y_i \preccurlyeq Y_j^*) = I(Y_i < Y_j^*) + 0.5I(Y_i = Y_j^*)$, where $I(\cdot)$ denotes the usual indicator function. With this notation, and with $\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}' = (A_j^* - A_i, (\mb{X^*}_j - \mb{X}_i)')$,model (\ref{final_PIM}) becomes 
\[
\E{I(Y \preccurlyeq Y^*)| A,A^*,\mb{X},\mb{X^*}} =   P(Y \preccurlyeq Y^* | A,A^*,\mb{X},\mb{X^*} ) = g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'\bbeta).
\]

The set $\mathcal{I}_n$ in (\ref{est_eq}) includes the pairs of $(A_i,\boldsymbol{X}_i)$ and $(A_j,\boldsymbol{X}_j)$ for which the PIM is defined. For model (\ref{final_PIM2}) this becomes $\mathcal{I}_n = \{i,j: A_i=0, A_j=1\}$.

Finally, $\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij};\bbeta)$ represents a vector-valued function involving the ``regressors'' $\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}$ and the parameter vector $\bbeta$:
\[
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij};\bbeta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial \bbeta} g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta) V^{-1}(g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta)),
\]
where $V^{-1}(g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta)) = g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta)(1-g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Z}'_{ij}\bbeta))$ is equal to the variance of the pseudo-observations $I_{ij}$, under the assumption that the PIM in \eqref{final_PIM} holds.

When the original data are uncorrelated, it can be shown that the pseudo-observations are only sparsely correlated and that, asymptotically, $\hat{\bbeta}$ follows a multivariate normal distribution with mean equal to the true $\bbeta$-vector $\bbeta_0$ and a variance covariance matrix that can be consistently estimated by the sandwich estimator
\begin{equation}
\label{PIM:vcoc}
    \hat{\Sigma}_{\hat{\bbeta_n}}= \left\{  \sum_{(i,j)\in \mathcal{I}_n}{\frac{\partial U_{ij}(\hat{\bbeta_n})}{\partial\beta^T}} \right\}^{-1}  \left\{ \sum_{(i,j)\in \mathcal{I}_n} \sum_{(k,l)\in \mathcal{I}_n} \phi_{ijkl} U_{ij}(\hat{\bbeta_n}) U^T_{kl}(\hat{\beta_n})  \right\}   \left\{  \sum_{(i,j)\in \mathcal{I}_n}{\frac{\partial U_{ij}(\hat{\bbeta_n})}{\partial\beta^T}} \right\}^{-1},
\end{equation}
where the double sum is taken over all pseudo-observations that either share the first or second original observation, i.e. $\phi_{ijkl}=1$ when $I_{ij}$ and $I_{kl}$ are correlated. For further theoretical details, the reader is referred to the dedicated papers \citep{ThaDen2012,DenTha2015}. The method is implemented in the "pim" R package available from CRAN \cite{Meys2020}. \cite{Jaspers2024} provide small-sample adjustments based on Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs), which allows for valid inference when the sample sizes of the two groups are low (i.e. up to $n=12$ per experimental group).

\subsection{Longitudinal Probabilistic Index Models}

When the original observations are not independent, but are rather derived from longitudinal profiles or contained within other clusters, the sparse correlation structure of the pseudo-observations is no longer a valid assumption. In this subsection, we show how the original framework can be modified, focusing on a longitudinal data setting where $n$ subjects ($S$) are followed over time ($T$), leading to observations $Y_{st},\ s=1,\ldots,n$ and $t=1,\ldots,n_T$. In order to allow for the estimation of both time effects and group effects, we consider the following PIM:

\begin{eqnarray*}
    \Phi^{-1}(\prob{Y \leqs Y^* \mid T,A,T^*,A^*})
    &=& \beta_0 + \beta_{T1} (T^*-T)A^*A + \beta_{T0} (T^*-T)(1-A^*)(1-A) \\
    & &   + \sum_{t=1}^{n_t}\beta_{At} (A^*-A)\indicat{T=T^*=t}.
\end{eqnarray*}
defined for all $\left((T,A,S), (T^*,A^*,S^*)\right) \in {\cal{X}}$ with ${\cal{X}}$ as follows
\begin{eqnarray*}
    {\cal{X}}
       &=& \big\{ \left((T,A,S), (T^*,A^*,S^*)\right) :  \\
       & & \left[S=S^* \text{ and } T<T^*\right] \text{ or}  \\
       & & \left[S\neq S^* \text{ and } T^*=T \text{ and } A<A^*\right]\big\}.
\end{eqnarray*}
It should be noted that $S^*=S$ implies $A^*=A$. As such, this model results in time-specific group effects, i.e.
\[
  \Phi(\beta_{At}) = \prob{Y \leqs Y^* \mid T=T^*=t, A=0, A^*=1},
\]
and group-specific time effects, i.e. with $i=$ 0 or 1 denoting the group
\[
  \Phi(\beta_{Ti}) = \prob{Y \leqs Y^* \mid S=S^*, A=A^*=i, T^*=T+1} .
\]

Under the assumption that observations from the same subjects are more related than observations from different subjects, it becomes clear that the sparse correlation structure mentioned in the subsection above is no longer valid. Indeed, considering for example that subject $S=1$ is observed at 4 time points (i.e., we observe $Y_{11}$, $Y_{12}$, $Y_{13}$, $Y_{14}$); the definition of ${\cal{X}}$ results in pseudo-observations $I_{11;12}, I_{11;13}, I_{11;14}, I_{11;23}, I_{11;24}, I_{11;34}$, where we have used the notation $I_{ss^*;ij}$ to denote a comparison of outcome $i$ of subject $s$ with outcome $j$ of subject $s^*$. Under sparse correlation, $I_{11;12}$ and $I_{11;34}$ would be independent as they are obtained from comparing different original observations ($Y_{11}$ vs. $Y_{12}$ and $Y_{13}$ vs $Y_{14}$). Nevertheless, as these original observations all come from the same subject, also these pseudo-observations can be considered to be related. As such, while the point estimates of the original PIM framework are still correct, an adjustment to the sandwich variance-covariance matrix is required. This can be achieved by reconsidering the definition of $\phi_{ijkl}$ in equation \eqref{PIM:vcoc}, which will now take the value of 1 when $(i,j)$ and $(k,l)$ correspond to observations from the same subject $S$ and zero otherwise. As will become clear in the simulation study of Section \ref{sim}, this minor modification has a huge (beneficial) impact on the standard errors of the parameter estimates and, as such, also on providing correct inference. 

\subsubsection{Small sample corrections}

In many studies, only limited sample sizes are available, which could lead to biased inference as the asymptotic normality assumption is not met. In addition, especially when using binary data such as our pseudo-observations, separation issues might arise when sample sizes are small. Following \cite{Jaspers2024}, the GEE toolbox can be used to improve the performance of PIMs in small samples through the combination of methods for fitting GEEs when dealing with non-nested clusters discussed by \cite{Miglioretti2004} and adjusting for small samples and separation issues following e.g. \cite{Morel2003} and \cite{Firth1993}. For the latter, \cite{Mondol2019} focus on corrections for longitudinal binary data.

For the non-nested clustering structure, it is important to note that two pseudo-observations can either share the same subject for the first outcome (i.e. $I_{ss^*;ij}$ and $I_{ss^{**};kl}$ ), the same subject for the second outcome (i.e. $I_{ss^*;ij}$ and $I_{s^{**}s^{*};kl}$ ) or both (i.e. $I_{ss^*;ij}$ and $I_{ss^{*};kl}$ ). We hence define a first cluster indicator, $C1$, which corresponds to the subject from which the first outcome is used and a second cluster indicator, $C2$, which corresponds to the subject from which the second outcome is used. To account for this non-nested clustering structure, \cite{Miglioretti2004} showed that correct inference can be obtained by fitting three GEEs under the independence assumption, where the first model uses the block independence defined by cluster indicator $C1$, model 2 uses the block independence defined by cluster indicator $C2$ and the third model uses a block independence defined by cluster indicator $C3$ that considers the unique combinations of $C1$ and $C2$. These three models provide the same point estimates (under an independence working correlation), but different variance covariance matrices, denoted, respectively by $V_1$, $V_2$ and $V_3$. The final variance-covariance matrix, reflecting the correct non-nested clustering structure, is composed by $V = V_1 +V_2 - V_3$. In the current work, GEE models 1-3 are fitted using the \cite{Ford2017} small sample adjustment and the penalized GEE approach as available in the geessbin R package \cite{Ishii2024}.


\subsubsection{Test for interaction}

After obtaining the parameter estimates and their corresponding (small-sample corrected) standard errors, reliable inference can be performed. In this paper, interest is in a test for interaction, to assess whether the probabilistic index comparing the experimental and control group remains constant over time, or whether the index changes

This interaction can be tested in two different ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item First, we could assess whether the group effect stays constant over time, i.e. $H_0: \beta_{A1} = \cdots = \beta_{An_t}$ vs. $H_1: \beta_{Ai} \neq \beta_{Aj}$ for at least one $i\neq j$
    \item As an alternative, we could assess whether time effect is the same in the two groups, i.e. $H_0: \beta_{T1}  = \beta_{T0}$ vs. $H_1: \beta_{T1}  \neq \beta_{T0}$.
\end{itemize}

0 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & \tfrac{5}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} \\
0 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & -\tfrac{1}{6} & \tfrac{5}{6}
\end{bmatrix}
\]
   which leads to 6 individual p-values, which are corrected for multiple testing using Holm adjustment. We reject $H_0$ if one of these adjusted p-values is smaller than 0.05. Alternatively, one can also perform global F-test using glht()
    
\end{itemize}

\section{Simulation Study}

We have set up two simulation studies:

\subsection{Gaussian random intercept model}

Generating model:

$$
Y_{ij} = \beta_1 * t_{ij} + \beta_2 * g_{i}+ \beta_3 *t_{ij}* g_{i} + \epsilon_{ij}  + b_{0i},
$$

$$
b_{0i}' \sim \mathcal{N}(0;\sigma_0^2)
$$

$$
\epsilon_{ij} \sim N(0,\sigma^2)
$$

We set $\sigma^2=1 and \sigma_0^2=1.5^2$ a


True parameters:
$$
\alpha_{Gt} = \frac{\beta_2}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1+\sigma^2_{0}}}
$$
